The governing principle

Everything below rests on one rule of thumb: behavioral data is strong; opinion data is weak. UX research observes what people do, not what they say or like — because for every 10 people who like a design, 10 will hate it and 10 won't care. Never pick a method first ("let's do a survey"); decide what data you need, then let the method follow.

The RouteMate mantra

"Will drivers use RouteMate to plan a multi-stop run faster than they do today?" is a behavioral question. "Do drivers like the new map?" is an opinion question — we will not build decisions on it.

1 Phase

Frame the problem

Before recruiting anyone, decide what you're researching and why. Skip this and you get "method-led" research that skims the surface. For RouteMate we start knowing only that a logistics company thinks drivers "waste time planning routes."

1.1

Two-Question Method Selector

When
At the very start, to decide what type of research you need.
Inputs
A rough problem statement + where you are in the lifecycle.
Output
One decision: field visit, usability test, or both.

All UX research answers one of two questions. Map your situation to the right one:

RouteMate example

We have no evidence drivers actually struggle with route planning — it's an assumption from management. Decision: run field research first (validate the problem: is planning really painful and costly?), then usability-test the RouteMate prototype later (validate the solution).

1.2

Anatomy of a Research Question

When
Before any study, to replace vague goals with one real question.
Inputs
The problem area + stakeholder goals.
Output
One sentence you could write on a whiteboard, ending in "?".

The research question is not a question you ask a participant. It's the underlying driver of the whole study. Score every candidate against six criteria — if it fails one, rewrite it.

The 6-criteria checklist (all must pass)
RQ is valid = Interesting ∧ Important ∧ Focused & specific ∧ Leads to a testable hypothesis ∧ Predicts from measurable data ∧ Advances knowledge beyond the obvious
Fail any one condition → the question is not yet ready.
RouteMate example

Weak objective: "We want to understand how drivers plan routes." (Not focused, not testable.)
Strong research question: "What causes multi-drop drivers to deviate from their planned route, and how much time does each deviation cost?" — specific, important, measurable (deviations, minutes lost), and testable.

1.3

The Four Problem-Definition Techniques

When
The brief is weak, or you have time to sharpen the problem.
Inputs
Initial brief/RFP + access to stakeholders.
Output
Refined problem, stakeholder-needs list, measurable sub-components, surfaced risks.
  1. Find out what other stakeholders need to know. List every stakeholder and meet them; ask what's been tried, why now, what success looks like, constraints, timeline, budget.
  2. Deconstruct the construct. Things like "usability" can't be observed directly — break them into measurable sub-components (read the literature; don't guess).
  3. Measure something. Ask what specifically to measure, the dependent variable, what to control, and whether it will convince the team.
  4. Shake out the issues. Run a cheap pre-pilot ("hit the grass to startle the snake") to surface hidden problems and missed stakeholders before spending budget.
Deconstructing the construct "usability" (ISO 9241-11)
Usability = Effectiveness + Efficiency + Satisfaction Effectiveness → task completion rate (% of runs planned correctly) Efficiency → time on task, error count (minutes to plan a 12-stop run) Satisfaction → post-task SEQ / SUS (secondary, weak evidence)
Now every sub-component is separately measurable → the study design writes itself.
RouteMate example

Stakeholder interviews reveal dispatch managers (want fewer support calls), drivers (want fewer taps while driving), and legal (phone-use-while-driving liability). The pre-pilot — shadowing one driver for a morning — surfaces a hidden constraint: drivers wear gloves, so tiny tap targets fail. That single finding reshapes the whole test plan.

1.4

Desk Research — the Venn Method

When
Before any primary research, to avoid re-discovering what's known.
Inputs
The product domain + access to internal reports/analytics.
Output
A synthesis of prior findings + explicit gaps.

Model the context of use as three circles — Users, Goals, Environments. The centre (your users, their goals, in context) is the rare sweet spot. Mine every overlap for existing evidence.

RouteMate example

No study exists on our exact app, but desk research finds: internal dispatch logs (goals + environment) showing the top re-route reasons, a driver-satisfaction survey (users + goals), and an academic ethnography of gig couriers (users + environment). Together they frame the field study — and flag that "failed delivery attempts" is the costliest gap.

2 Phase

Plan & recruit

"Our app is for everyone" is how products fail. Pick which users teach you the most, fastest — then recruit them with a screener that filters on behavior, not demographics.

2.1

The 2×2 User-Group Grid

When
The team says "everyone," or you don't know who to research first.
Inputs
The team + sticky notes.
Output
Gold / silver / bronze user groups.

Each person writes ≥5 candidate user groups (one per sticky). Plot each on two axes and read off the priority.

Priority score for a user group
Priority = Learning × Access where Learning = how much this group teaches about our riskiest assumptions Access = how easily we can reach & schedule them
Both high → GOLD (start now) · high-learning/low-access → SILVER (backlog) · low-learning/high-access → BRONZE (later) · low/low → SKIP.
RouteMate example

Groups generated: city multi-drop couriers, long-haul drivers, weekend gig drivers, dispatch managers. Gold = city multi-drop couriers (feel the pain daily, easy to shadow). Silver = long-haul (high learning, hard to schedule) → backlog. Skip = weekend gig drivers (low learning, and we'd burn budget).

2.2

The Perfect Participant Screener (8 rules)

When
Recruiting anyone — get representative, articulate people; avoid no-shows.
Inputs
Behavioral profile of target users + recruiting channel.
Output
A tested screener + a confirmed roster.
#RuleRouteMate application
1Screen for behaviors, not demographicsClassify each candidate high/low on digital skills & domain (driving) knowledge, not age.
2Ask precise questionsNot "are you techy?" but "do you use nav apps daily, and do you edit stops mid-route?"
3Identify unsuitable candidates earlyFunnel: exclusion questions first. Open "where do you work?" (avoids "faking good").
4Get value-for-money participantsFor think-aloud, screen out inarticulate candidates via one open question.
5Manage expectationsState: interviewed alone, recorded, cash on attendance, NDA — not a focus group.
6Pilot-test the screenerRun on people you want / don't want; get stakeholder sign-off so the sample can't be dismissed later.
7Avoid no-showsConfirm day-before, text on the day; recruit paid floaters; double-recruit critical slots.
8Brief the recruiting companyWalk the actual recruiter through the screener; flag where flexibility is allowed.
The two behavioral screening variables (web/app projects)
Recruit a MIX across the 2×2 of: Digital skills {low, high} × Domain knowledge {low, high} → ensures you see novices AND experts, not just one corner.
RouteMate example

Screener classifies drivers as high/low digital-skill (uses nav apps unaided vs. asks family) and high/low domain (does ≥15 drops/day vs. occasional). We recruit 2 from each cell (8 total) so RouteMate is tested by both a fumble-fingered veteran and a tech-savvy newbie.

3 Phase

Conduct the research

Now you collect the strong, behavioral evidence — first by observing drivers in the wild, then by watching them attempt real tasks on the RouteMate prototype without you getting in the way.

3.1

Field Visit / Contextual Research

When
Answering "who are our users & what are they trying to do?"
Inputs
Recruited users in their real environment.
Output
Observations feeding personas, journey maps, task models.
  1. Go where the action happens (the cab, the depot) — "safari, not the zoo." Observe natural behavior in its natural environment.
  2. Record what people do, not just what they say.
  3. Capture the six things every model needs: goals, task workflow, mental models, tools, environments, and the terminology people use.
  4. Use it to validate the problem hypothesis: is this a real, serious problem — or an itch nobody will pay to scratch?
RouteMate example

Riding along, we see a courier "plan his route around lunch so he's near a bakery at noon" — a real goal invisible in a lab. We also observe he re-sequences stops by memory at each traffic light (dangerous) and calls dispatch when a customer isn't home. Terminology: he says "drops," never "deliveries"; "the run," never "the route." RouteMate's copy must match.

3.2

Writing Test Tasks (not questions)

When
Preparing a usability test.
Inputs
Top user tasks (ideally from field research).
Output
A realistic task/scenario set.

There are no questions in usability — you write a list of tasks. A good task is realistic, measurable, and does not reveal the solution path.

Don't (opinion-seeking)

"Do you find it easy to add a delivery stop?" — invites an opinion and leads the witness.

Do (behavioral task)

"You've just been told parcel #4402 for 14 Oak Street must be delivered before 11am. Add it to today's run and make sure it happens in time." — realistic, measurable (did they succeed? how long?), not leading.

3.3

Moderating: The Five Mistakes to Avoid

When
Running any moderated usability session.
Inputs
Participant, tasks, recording.
Output
Clean behavioral observations with minimal moderator bias.
MistakeThe fix
1. Talking too muchEmbrace silence. Intro ≤5 min. If you must speak, only: "Tell me more about that."
2. Explaining the designNever "what the developers meant was…" Instead: "Tell me what you're doing right now."
3. Answering questionsBoomerang: answer a question with a question — "How do you think you'd do that?"
4. Interviewing not testingSit behind & to one side; avoid eye contact. Save questions for a post-test interview.
5. Soliciting opinionsNever "which do you prefer?" Usability = what works best, not what they like.
RouteMate example

A driver asks, "Where's the button to reorder stops?" The novice moderator points to it (Mistake 3). The correct move: "Where would you expect to find it?" — then watch where he actually looks. He taps the map three times before finding the list — revealing this is a critical, not cosmetic, problem.

3.4

Usability Expert Review

When
Efficient problem-finding without recruiting users, or before testing.
Inputs
The interface + a data-driven description of users & tasks + 3–5 reviewers.
Output
A consolidated, severity-rated list of predicted problems.

Fix the four classic errors: (1) take the user's perspective, not yours ("it doesn't matter what you like"); (2) use 3–5 reviewers, not one; (3) use technology-specific guidelines; (4) rate which problems matter.

Why several reviewers — problems found rises with reviewer count
Problems found = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ p = fraction of problems one reviewer finds (≈ 0.35) n = number of reviewers n=1 → ~35% n=3 → ~73% n=5 → ~88%
Same math underlies "5 users find ~85% of problems." Different reviewers catch different problem types.
RouteMate example

Three reviewers each first write the courier's goals/tasks, then inspect independently: a logistics-domain reviewer flags that "estimated arrival" ignores loading time; a visual reviewer flags 28px tap targets (fails for gloved hands); an IA reviewer flags that "Runs" and "History" are indistinguishable. Merged & severity-rated → one prioritized backlog.

3.5

Controlling Researcher Bias

When
Every study — bias favors the researcher's hypothesis ~70% of the time.
Inputs
The protocol + data loggers.
Output
A bias-controlled protocol + defensible dataset.
  • Interaction bias (Clever Hans): stay neutral in tone, posture, expression; don't nod toward the sponsor's design; stay out of the participant's sightline; review your own session video for "tells."
  • Recording/interpreting bias: fix data-logging codes and pass/fail criteria beforehand; use ≥2 loggers for inter-scorer reliability; record verbatim, not interpretation.
  • Sponsorship/confirmation bias: "you shouldn't care what the outcome is — only that your data is bulletproof." Report negatives straight.
Caught on tape

RouteMate moderator stayed neutral introducing the old app, but leaned in and nodded while introducing the new prototype — a Clever Hans tell caught only by reviewing the recording. Fixed by scripting a flat, identical intro for both.

4 Phase

Analyze

A usability test finds problems, not solutions. Here you turn a fire-hose of raw observations into a ranked, defensible list of fixes.

4.1

Observations → Insights → Hypotheses → Solutions

When
After a usability test, to convert observations into fixes.
Inputs
Raw observations, sticky notes, the team, a whiteboard.
Output
Top-3 insights, each with hypotheses and a minimal testable change.
Keep data clean

An observation is objective ("3 of 8 drivers tapped the map to reorder stops"). It is NOT an interpretation ("the reorder control is unclear") and NOT a solution ("add a drag handle"). Those come later.

RouteMate example — one thread end to end

Observations: 6/8 drivers never used the "⋮" more-menu; 5/8 tapped the map to reorder.
Insight (headline): "Drivers don't recognise the ⋮ as a control — they treat the map as the interface."
Hypotheses: (a) they think ⋮ is branding; (b) it's too far top-right where gloved thumbs don't reach; (c) they assume reordering happens by dragging pins.
Smallest solution to test next: replace ⋮ with a visible "Reorder stops" button on the list, and enable pin-dragging on the map. Each hypothesis maps to a different tweak — A/B in the next sprint.

4.2

Severity Decision Tree (3 questions)

When
A test returns 100+ issues and the team needs to know what to fix first.
Inputs
The list of problems.
Output
Every problem tagged Critical / Serious / Medium / Low, transparently.

Ask three yes/no questions of each problem and walk the tree. No gut feel — a developer can't argue with a repeatable rule.

Severity interpretation & action
CRITICAL → users can't/won't finish a common task → fix urgently SERIOUS → significantly slows / forces a workaround → fix ASAP MEDIUM → frustrating but doesn't block completion → next BAU update LOW → cosmetic (typo, alignment) → minor (but too many = brand damage)

5 Phase

Act on the findings

Research that doesn't change the product is theatre. Package what you learned into a lightweight, updatable user model and secure named owners for each fix.

5.1

The Agile 2D-Sketch Persona

When
You need a lightweight, updatable user model — not a glossy, "cast-in-concrete" persona.
Inputs
Completed research (you must have done it), flip-chart, Sharpies, sticky notes, the team.
Output
A disposable, wall-mounted conversation-starter.

Split flip-chart paper into four quadrants; fill each with one item per sticky note. Prioritise, discuss, post on a wall, update as you learn. Do not polish it — "it's the process that matters, not the beauty of the artifact."

5.2

Getting the Team to Act

  1. Run a one-day research + design workshop to transition findings and solutions to the team.
  2. Provide specific, actionable recommendations — you're a full team member, not just a critic.
  3. Agree accountability: a named owner for every recommendation.
  4. Promote iterative design: schedule a re-test of the revised prototype.
  5. Present clear next steps; chair the design meetings, don't just attend.

6 Phase

Improve your practice

The last framework turns the researcher into a better researcher — run it after every stage, not just at the end.

6.1

Structured Reflection (with 5 Whys)

When
Continuously, after each stage of a project.
Inputs
A just-completed activity + a journal.
Output
A dated entry that feeds future case studies and skills.
  1. What did you do? (date, method, sample, duration)
  2. Why that activity rather than another?
  3. What went well? 2–3 specifics.
  4. What went badly? 2–3 specifics.
  5. Analyze each point with the 5 Whys — drill to the root cause.
  6. Integrate & ask "What if?" — what will you do differently next time?
5 Whys on a RouteMate session that overran
Problem: only 3 of 5 tasks fit in the 60-min session. Why? → the intro ran 15 minutes. Why? → I over-explained the prototype. Why? → I was nervous about silence. Why? → I hadn't rehearsed a tight script. Why? → no pre-pilot of the session timing. ROOT CAUSE → skipped session pilot. FIX → pilot timing; cap intro at 5 min (see 3.3, Mistake 1).
The whole loop, in one line

Frame → Plan → Conduct → Analyze → Act → Improve — and back again. The connective tissue across all six phases is the governing principle: collect behavioral evidence, prioritise by strength of evidence, and let the data — not opinion — decide.
